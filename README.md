Design rationale. The single-country design is deliberate. Comparable international assessment data are sparse: PISA is observed every three years, TIMSS every four and PIRLS every five, so any national series has five to eight points. A forecasting method that needs long histories cannot be applied to such data. A zero-shot foundation model can, in principle, because it is pretrained on a very large corpus and needs no training data from the target series. Google’s publications describe TimesFM as pretrained on roughly 100 billion time points, mostly Google Trends and Wikipedia page-view series, and report near state-of-the-art zero-shot accuracy on unseen benchmark datasets without task-specific training (Das et al., 2024; Google Research, 2024). The Netherlands was therefore chosen as a national example, with a verified record in all three PISA domains plus TIMSS and PIRLS, to test TimesFM in exactly the data-scarce conditions for which zero-shot forecasting is proposed. Two caveats frame the test. “Sparse” in the TimesFM publications refers mainly to series with many missing or zero values, and the benchmark datasets they report are much longer than five to eight observations, so forecasting very short, triennial series is an extension of the published evaluations rather than something they establish. The test is also a fair one only if simple benchmarks are included, which is why linear, naïve, drift and mean forecasts are reported alongside TimesFM.
The paper addresses three questions:
    • RQ1. How have Dutch mathematics, reading and science scores changed across the available PISA record (2003–2025), relative to the OECD average where that is available?
    • RQ2. Is the decline well described by a single linear trend, or do the series show a change in pace?
    • RQ3. In data-scarce conditions of this kind, how well does Google’s TimesFM foundation model, used zero-shot, forecast the next one or two assessment cycles compared with a linear trend and other simple benchmarks?
